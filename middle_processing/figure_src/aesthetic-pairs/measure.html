Why cities need more trees, and why they keep losing them

Street trees are the cheapest air conditioning a city can buy. Most cities are still cutting down more than they plant.

By Maren Holt · 3 November 2026 · 14 min read

On a hot afternoon in July, the difference between a shaded block and an unshaded one is not subtle. You feel it through the soles of your shoes before you see it on a thermometer. Asphalt that has been in the sun since morning radiates heat back up long after dusk, and the people who live on those streets sleep worse, breathe worse and, in the worst heat waves, die in greater numbers than their neighbours a few blocks away.

None of this is news to the people who manage urban forests. What has changed is the size of the gap. As summers have grown longer, the blocks with the fewest trees have become measurably hotter, and they are rarely the blocks where city budgets for planting are spent. Planting follows complaints, and complaints follow the people with the most time to write them.

A tree takes a decade or more to become useful shade. In that time it needs water through its first dry summers, a pit large enough for its roots, and protection from the trucks, utility crews and renovations that kill young street trees long before they mature. Cities count trees planted. Very few count the ones still standing ten years later.

The fix is not mysterious. It is a maintenance budget that lasts as long as the trees do, planting targets set street by street rather than city-wide, and a simple public map of where the canopy is thinnest. The cities that have done all three have reversed their losses within a single mayoral term.
